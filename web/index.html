The orchestrator

Six specialists reasoning over one project.

Each is a separately scoped reasoning step that reads the outputs of the ones before it. They stay distinct modules rather than one prompt doing everything — so a real domain model can replace any single specialist without restructuring the system.

01 · Architecture

Space and adjacency

Turns the brief into a room list with dimensions and adjacency logic — which rooms must touch, which must not, and where circulation runs.

02 · Structural

Load paths, advisory

Flags load-bearing placement and span limits in plain language. Explicitly advisory — a licensed engineer signs off, not the model.

03 · Interior

Materials and light

Palette, finishes and furniture layout per room, reasoned against the stated style and the climate the building actually sits in.

04 · Sustainability

Orientation and energy

Material choices scored for the local climate, with a projected energy profile rather than a generic green badge.

05 · Cost

Quantities, then price

Derives quantities from the plan and prices them against published schedules of rates. The model selects items; it never authors a rate.

06 · Compliance

Approvability

FSI, setbacks, height, parking and fire egress against one pilot city's bylaws — plus the NOCs this project will likely need.

One real brief

Demonstrated end to end on a single concrete project.

Not "design any house." One believable brief with named constraints, run through the whole pipeline — a specific project is far more convincing than a generic capability claim, and costs nothing extra to build.

3D and file formats

The 3D is massing, on purpose.

Switch the plan above to 3D massing and the same room footprints extrude into block volumes, one storey at a time. Drag to orbit, click a room to jump straight into designing it. It is deliberately not a photoreal render — at the decision stage you need to read the building, not admire it, and a render takes hours you would rather spend on the budget.

To actually move walls — drag rooms, pull handles to resize, add and delete spaces, across floors — open Synapse Studio. Everything you change there recomputes the cost, the quantities, the bylaw checks and the readiness score as you drag.

What "AI" actually means here

Recommendation is weighted scoring over structured data, not a trained model. Cost is arithmetic over a rate card, not a prediction. Compliance is a rule checklist, not legal validation. Readiness is a weighted composite of the other three.

That is a feature, not an apology. Every number on this page can be traced to an input — which is what a homeowner deserves when the answer decides whether they can afford to build.

Questions

The ones worth asking.

Is this trying to replace Revit or AutoCAD?

No. Those are drafting and modelling tools and they are very good at it. Synapse sits earlier, at the decision stage, with a narrower claim: when you change a decision, it understands the consequences. The output hands off into professional tools rather than competing with them.

How is this different from an AI that generates floor plans?

Plan generation, cost estimation and compliance checking are all available separately today. The value here is the relationship between them — one shared project model, so a change in one place is never silently inconsistent with the other three.

Are the compliance checks an approval?

They are preliminary checks, labelled as such everywhere they appear. Only your municipality approves a plan. What Synapse does is stop you discovering an FSI problem after you have paid for drawings.

Are the structural notes engineering advice?

They are not, and the product says so everywhere they appear. They are sanity checks that help you ask a structural engineer better questions.

Who makes the final call?

The architect, engineer or homeowner. Always. Synapse produces the connected analysis that makes the decision faster and better informed; it does not make the decision.
